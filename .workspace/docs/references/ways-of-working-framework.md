# Ways of working: a conceptual framework

This document defines *way of working* (WoW) as a general concept. It does not set a process for any project, prescribe tools, or authorize changes. A project's operating policy must make its own choices and explain why they fit its circumstances.

## Definition

A WoW is the set of decisions through which a team or project turns needs into delivered, verifiable outcomes. It describes how work is chosen and carried out, who makes decisions, what evidence permits progress, and how the process changes as the team learns.

A list of commands or tools is not a WoW. Nor is a sequence of gates without reasons for those gates. Procedures can change while the underlying decisions and criteria remain the same.

## Dimensions

1. **Development approach.** How much is decided up front, and how much is discovered during the work? The approach may be predictive, adaptive, or hybrid, and may vary with the kind of work and its risks.
2. **Life cycle.** How does a need become a verified outcome? Which decisions allow work to advance? Delivery may happen once, in several releases, or continuously. Delivery cadence is distinct from the mechanism used to publish an individual change.
3. **Practices and techniques.** Teams may estimate, plan, prioritize, manage dependencies and risk, check quality, and measure progress. These practices earn their place by helping the team make decisions; no metric or ceremony is required merely because it appears in a framework.
4. **Roles, artifacts, and interactions.** Who proposes, decides, builds, reviews, and accepts? What information must be kept, and when do people coordinate? Responsibilities should remain understandable even if tools or role names change.

These dimensions affect one another. A phase needs a purpose. A quality check needs a risk to address and an outcome to verify. A decision needs an identifiable owner.

## Choosing and adapting a WoW

There is no universally correct WoW. Relevant conditions include uncertainty about requirements, the cost of changing completed work, the impact of failure, external obligations, and the team's ability to coordinate.

- If work is expected to reveal new requirements, smaller deliveries and opportunities to reconsider scope may help.
- If rework or failure is costly, consequential decisions and the evidence needed to accept them may need to be settled earlier.
- If conditions differ across kinds of work, explicit criteria can determine which practices apply instead of forcing every change through the same path.

These are considerations, not automatic rules. A project must say which conditions trigger a different path and who can approve an exception.

## Questions a documented WoW should answer

Someone new to a project should be able to answer these questions without reading instructions written for another role:

1. What outcome is sought, and how are needs submitted and prioritized?
2. What is a unit of work, and what must be known before it begins?
3. When is direct implementation appropriate, and when is prior design, investigation, or approval needed?
4. Which risks matter, and how do they change the required checks?
5. Who sets scope, reviews the result, and authorizes delivery or an exception?
6. What proves that a result works and has reached its intended destination?
7. How often is work delivered, how is progress observed, and what happens when work is blocked?
8. When and how is the WoW itself revised in light of results and friction?

For each practice, the description should state its purpose, when it applies, who is responsible, what allows work to continue, and what happens on failure or exception. It may name a tool without becoming a manual for that tool.

## Three scales

The term can apply at different scales. For an individual, it concerns the ability to recognize and choose approaches. For a project, it concerns the choice of approach, life cycle, and governance. For a team, it concerns the choice and evolution of day-to-day practices. Competency frameworks, project-management guides, and process catalogs can inform these different choices; none makes the specific decisions for a team.

## Why document it

An explicit WoW helps people coordinate expectations, interpret progress using shared criteria, and review whether practices still serve their purpose. Improvement requires observing outcomes and problems, choosing a change, assigning responsibility, and checking its effect. A written WoW is not evidence that a team follows it; its claims should be testable against actual work.
